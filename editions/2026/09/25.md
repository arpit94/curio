## Paper of the Day

**[Synthetic Hospital: An Open, Verifiable, Physician-Validated Longitudinal EHR Benchmark](http://arxiv.org/abs/2609.30027v1)** — Christine Park, Valerie Chen and Tim Dettmers tackle a basic problem in clinical AI: real EHR data can't be shared openly, and it has no verifiable ground truth, because a chart only records what clinicians chose to write down. Synthetic Hospital is built entirely from public medical-education material. It has 1,268 longitudinal patients and 5,602 encounters, and every diagnosis, finding and temporal relation is grounded in ICD-10-CM, SNOMED CT and LOINC, with provenance back to its source. It runs behind a simulated EHR with standard interoperability APIs, role-based access and a function-calling interface, so agents can be tested the way they would actually be deployed. In a blinded review, physicians told its charts apart from real ones only 53% of the time. The best of 10 frontier and open models reached a severity-weighted F1 of 0.73 on longitudinal problem lists. That matches the average of seven physicians but falls well short of the best one (0.89), and models missed about half of the clinically relevant findings when summarizing charts. **Why it matters:** It gives you an open, realistic sandbox with EHR-shaped APIs for testing clinical agents and retrieval pipelines, which is exactly the eval gap health-AI teams run into.

## Deep Dive: wildcard

**[Why is the liver so weirdly regenerative?](https://dynomight.substack.com/p/liver)** — Dynomight, known for patient explainers, takes on an old puzzle. You can remove about two-thirds of a mammal's liver and it grows back to roughly the right size within weeks, while the heart, brain and kidneys barely regenerate at all. The piece looks at the likely reasons. The liver is the body's detox filter, so it takes constant chemical damage, which creates strong evolutionary pressure to repair it. Its mature cells, hepatocytes, can re-enter the cell cycle instead of depending on a small pool of stem cells. And it seems to sense its own size relative to the body, growing until function is restored and then stopping. That last point is the deep question: how does an organ know when it is done? The HN thread (164 comments) adds clinical detail on living-donor transplants and on where regeneration fails, as in cirrhosis. ([discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49832938)) **Why it matters:** Beyond being plain interesting, the liver is a biological version of a self-healing distributed system: redundant capacity, local repair and a feedback loop that knows its target size. It also explains why living-donor liver transplants work.
